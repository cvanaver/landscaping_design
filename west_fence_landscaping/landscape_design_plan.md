# West Fence Line Landscape Plan

A modern, low-maintenance perennial border along ~46 linear feet of stepped wood privacy fence on the western boundary of the backyard at 1013 Colfax St, Evanston, IL. Designed for an east-facing bed on an excessively drained sandy ridge with a small mature pear tree at the north jut corner.

This plan supersedes the original install spec. The soil profile in the original document (clay-over-rock) was wrong. USDA Soil Data Access API data shows this lot is on the Urban land–Psamments complex — old glacial Lake Chicago beach ridge sand. The planting approach, watering schedule, and amendment strategy in this document are written for that soil reality.

---

## 1. Site Summary

**Location:** 1013 Colfax St, Evanston, IL — USDA Zone 6a (lake-moderated; the Evanston shoreline corridor runs ~half a zone warmer than inland Cook County).

**Bed orientation:** East-facing. Fence runs north–south along the west property line. Bed sits on the east (yard) side of the fence; neighbor is to the west. Plants get morning and early-afternoon sun, with fence shade arriving in mid-to-late afternoon. Net: ~5–7 hours of direct sun, all on the gentle side of the day. Reads as "high part sun / easy full sun" for plant selection.

**Soil profile (USDA Soil Data Access):**

| Depth | Texture | pH | Sand | Silt | Clay |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0–10" | Loam | 6.7 | 35% | 40% | 25% |
| 10–38" | Sand | 6.8 | 95% | 3% | 2% |
| 38–60" | Sand | 7.0 | 93% | 5% | 2% |

- Map unit: **Urban land–Psamments complex, nearly level**
- Drainage class: **excessively drained**

- The 0–10" loam cap is accumulated lawn topsoil over the native beach-ridge sand

**Hydrology:** Site sits on a small plateau just below a ridge crest (not in a trough). Briefly floods in spring during snowmelt and heavy rain events. Water recedes within hours to a day. This is a sandy plateau, not a wet site — the chosen plants tolerate this pattern.

**Fence geometry:** Stepped wood privacy fence with lattice top. Three runs:

### Why this layout

- **Straight front edge.** The fence steps, the bed doesn't. The front edge runs in one continuous straight line for the full 46 ft, aligned with the easternmost face of the jut. Cleaner sightline, easier mowing, no inside-corner mulch traps.
- **Deep side alcoves, shallow middle.** Where the fence sits back at the property line, the bed is ~63" deep (back row + front row + space between). Where the fence juts east, the bed is only ~30" deep (front row only). The two alcoves give the grasses room to breathe — they sit ~6" off the fence as planned, with ~33" of mulched space between them and the flower line in front.
- **Grasses on the property-line sections only.** The 4–5 ft Karl Foerster plumes screen the neighbor where it matters and stay out of the jutting middle section, which would otherwise read as a tall mass poking into the yard near the kids' play area.
- **Single continuous front line.** Coneflowers and geraniums share a single 14"-spaced row across the full 549". The plant identity changes under the pear canopy (Echinacea → Geranium) but the line stays unbroken. From the deck or lawn the bed reads as one ribbon, not three sections.
- **Pear becomes a feature, not a problem.** The geranium zone under the pear canopy turns the tree into a deliberate "shady corner" rather than a dead spot where coneflowers struggle.

### Seasonal payoff

- **Spring (April–May):** Geraniums leaf out and bloom pink/magenta in late May. Pear tree blooms above (depending on cultivar).
- **Early summer (June):** Karl Foerster sends up pinkish plumes. Geraniums still blooming. Coneflowers budding.
- **Mid-late summer (July–September):** Coneflowers in full bloom along the entire sunny front line. Grass plumes turn golden. Goldfinches working seedheads in August.
- **Fall (October–November):** Grass plumes go wheat-tan. Coneflower seedheads turn black, structural. Geranium foliage flushes red-bronze.
- **Winter:** Karl Foerster wheat plumes against snow. Coneflower seedheads feed birds. Geraniums hold semi-evergreen rosettes at ground level.

## 3. Plant Selection

Three species. No nursery decisions deferred to install day.

### Back row (alcove sections only)

**Karl Foerster Feather Reed Grass** — *Calamagrostis × acutiflora* 'Karl Foerster'

- Height: 4–5 ft in bloom; 2.5–3 ft foliage. On sand at this site expect 3.5–4.5 ft plumes (slightly shorter than on loam).
- Spread: 18–24"
- Sun: handles 4+ hours; thrives in the morning + early-afternoon exposure of this bed
- Bloom: pinkish plumes in June, fading to wheat-tan that holds all winter
- Sterile cultivar — will not self-seed
- Hardy to Zone 4; rock-solid in Zone 6a
- **Sand tolerance:** Excellent. Native range includes lean European meadow soils; tolerates sandy, gravelly conditions once established. One of the better ornamental grass choices for this specific soil.
- Standard #1 (1-gal) nursery pot

### Front line (sunny portions — north alcove, south alcove, and middle jut)

**Echinacea purpurea** (purple coneflower) — straight species or cultivar **'Magnus'**

- Height: 2.5–4 ft
- Bloom: late June through September, pink-purple daisy
- Sun: 4+ hrs; this bed is ideal
- Drought tolerance: high once established (deep taproot reaches the moister sub-sand)
- Native to tallgrass prairie and sandy/rocky outcrops — a near-perfect ecological match for your soil
- pH 5.5–7.5 (handles your 6.7–7.2 range easily)
- Winter interest: black seedheads, goldfinch food

**Cultivar choice:** Straight species *E. purpurea* (most reliable, longest-lived) or **'Magnus'** (more uniform habit, sturdier stems, still long-lived). Avoid:
- 'PowWow Wild Berry' (good plant, but shorter than what fits this bed scale)
- Modern fancy-color hybrids ('Cheyenne Spirit', 'Cleopatra', orange/yellow/double-flowered varieties) — bred for one-season nursery appeal, short-lived, often die out by year 3
- *Rudbeckia hirta* sold as "black-eyed Susan annual color" — biennial, will not return reliably

### Front line (under pear canopy — north end of the bed)

**Geranium macrorrhizum** (Bigroot cranesbill) — 'Bevan's Variety' or straight species

- Height: 10–12"; mat-forming spread by rhizomes
- Bloom: pink-magenta in late May / early June, before coneflowers start
- Foliage: scalloped, fragrant when crushed (deer-resistant), semi-evergreen in Zone 6a, flushes red-bronze in fall
- Sun: full sun to part shade; tolerates dry shade under tree roots — the workhorse choice for the pear zone
- **Why this plant for this site:** Native to limestone-cliff and beach habitats in the Balkans. Tolerates dry sandy soil with root competition better than almost any other shade perennial. Spreads to fill its zone within 2–3 years.
- Standard quart or #1 (1-gal) nursery pot

**Cultivar choice:** 'Bevan's Variety' (deeper magenta blooms, most vigorous) or 'Spessart' (softer pink). Both are reliable. Avoid 'Ingwersen's Variety' — least vigorous of the named cultivars.

### Plants explicitly rejected for this site

| Plant | Why not |
|---|---|
| *Rudbeckia fulgida* 'Goldsturm' | Wants moist prairie soil; fibrous shallow roots dry out fast on excessively drained sand |
| *Anemone × hybrida* (Japanese anemone) | Wants moist humusy soil; spends life looking thirsty on dry sand |
| *Heuchera* (coral bells) | Marginal on sand without amendment; the geranium does this job better at this site |
| *Astilbe* | Needs consistent moisture; won't survive without massive amendment and irrigation |
| *Schizachyrium scoparium* (Little Bluestem) | Considered as an alternative grass; rejected because it would dilute the design's vertical-grass-as-feature concept |

## 5. Soil & Planting Protocol — Sandy Ridge Edition

The original install plan was written for clay-over-rock. Almost every soil instruction in it is now wrong. This section replaces it.

### 5.1 What you are *not* doing

- ❌ Digging bar / rock bar — not needed
- ❌ Rock-removal protocol — there are no rocks
- ❌ "Shift the plant if you hit a rock" — irrelevant
- ❌ Stockpiling excavated rock — moot
- ❌ Worrying about a "bathtub" effect from amended planting holes — that's a clay-soil problem. On sand the opposite is true; amended holes retain water *better* than the surrounding substrate.

This is a much easier dig than the original plan assumed. One person with a shovel and a wheelbarrow can do all 41 holes in an afternoon.

### 5.2 The actual problem: water and nutrient retention

On clay you fight drainage. On sand you fight the lack of it:

- Available water capacity of sand is ~1/3 of clay
- Cation exchange capacity (CEC) of pure sand is near zero — fertilizer leaches straight through
- Sand heats up and cools down fast — bigger root-zone temperature swings
- Surface evaporation is high — no moist crust forms after rain

The fix: organic matter, lots of it, mixed into the planting zone. Building a sponge in the root zone.

### 5.3 Compost: what, how much

**What to buy** (in order of preference):
1. **Aged mushroom compost** — fine texture, holds water, slow-release nitrogen, fungally active. Bagged or by the yard.
2. **Aged leaf mold** — best long-term soil builder, hard to source in volume.
3. **Composted aged manure** (cow or horse, well-aged) — fine; avoid fresh hot manure.
4. ❌ Cheap bagged "compost" from big-box stores — mostly bark fines, low CEC. Skip.
5. Peat moss alone is not a bulk amendment — acidic, decomposes fast, contributes little nutrient. Fine as a minor (10%) ingredient.

**How much:**
- ~0.4 cu ft of compost per planting hole × 41 holes ≈ 17 cu ft
- Plus surface incorporation in the pear zone: another ~10 cu ft
- **Total: ~30 cu ft, or ~15 bags of 2 cu ft, or ~1 yard delivered**

### 5.4 Per-hole protocol

**Karl Foerster grasses (back row):**
- Hole: **16" wide × 14" deep** (deeper than v1 spec to encourage downward rooting into the moister sub-sand)
- Backfill mix: **50% excavated soil + 40% compost + 10% leaf mold or peat moss**
- One tablespoon of slow-release organic fertilizer (Plant-tone, Espoma 4-3-3, or similar) per hole, mixed into the backfill
- Set crown **½" above grade** (see 5.6 below — spring flooding adjustment)
- Backfill, firm gently with hands, water in immediately

**Echinacea coneflowers:**
- Hole: **10" wide × 10" deep**
- Same backfill mix as above
- One teaspoon of slow-release organic fertilizer per hole
- Crown ½" above grade
- Backfill, firm, water in

**Geranium macrorrhizum:**
- Hole: **10" wide × 10" deep**
- Same backfill mix
- **No starter fertilizer** for geraniums — they don't need it and over-fed plants lose foliage compactness
- Crown ½" above grade
- Backfill, firm, water in
- Expect to encounter some pear roots while digging. Clean-cut any small roots (<½" diameter) with sharp pruners; the tree regenerates fine from these.

### 5.5 Bed-wide amendment: hybrid approach

Hole-only amendment works on sand, but plants stay locked into amended pockets. For best results:

- **Broadfork the pear zone** (~10 ft of front line). Spread 2–3" compost across the surface, then work in with a broadfork to 6–8" depth. Do NOT till — broadforking aerates without inverting and destroying soil structure. This gives the geraniums a continuous improved root zone to spread into.
- **Hole-amend the rest of the bed.** Coneflowers don't spread; they don't need bed-wide amendment.

This is about 1 extra hour of work for materially better long-term performance in the pear zone.

### 5.6 Crown placement — the spring flooding adjustment

Because the site briefly ponds in spring during snowmelt and heavy rain:

- **Set all plant crowns ½" above surrounding bed grade**, not level with it
- Mound backfill soil gently up to the crown, sloping down to bed level
- Why: a crown set even ¼" below grade will collect standing water during spring melt events, which is when crown rot starts. ½" proud lets surface water drain past instead of pooling.

This is the single most important install detail for this site.

### 5.7 Mulch

- **Material:** Shredded hardwood mulch (oak/maple bark). NOT dyed-red cypress. NOT cocoa hulls (toxic to dogs). NOT playground wood chips.
- **Depth:** **3" minimum** after settling. Sand needs the evaporation barrier and temperature buffer more than clay does.
- **Coverage:** ~3 cu yards if mulching the entire bed footprint (the deep alcoves plus the front line strip and the pear-zone broadfork area). About 18–20 bags of 2 cu ft if buying bagged.
- **Crown clearance:** Pull mulch 2" away from the base of every plant. No volcano mulching.

### 5.8 Fertilizer reality check

Most sandy-soil perennial beds get over-fertilized by anxious gardeners who think "sand = needs more fertilizer." Both coneflowers and ornamental grasses produce floppy weak growth and reduced bloom when over-fed.

The schedule:
- **At planting:** one tablespoon slow-release organic per grass hole; one teaspoon per coneflower. None for geraniums.
- **Spring of year 2:** light handful per grass, half-handful per coneflower, scratched into surface mulch. None for geraniums.
- **Year 3+:** none, unless growth is visibly weak.

Never use synthetic 10-10-10 or any high-nitrogen lawn fertilizer near the bed.

## 6. Installation Sequence

### Day 0 — Prep (afternoon, 2 hrs)

1. Mark the new **straight front edge** with mason's line stretched between stakes. Step back, sight the line from the deck and from the dome area. Adjust until it looks straight from the viewing angles that matter.
2. Cut the edge with a flat spade — clean vertical cut, 4" deep.
3. Pull sod from inside the new bed footprint. Compost the sod or flip it grass-down in the bottom of large grass holes.
4. Hand-weed any remaining roots.
5. If broadforking the pear zone: spread 2–3" compost across the ~10 ft pear-canopy strip and work in with broadfork to 6–8" depth.

### Day 1 morning — Layout (45 min)

6. Use a tape measure and marking flags to mark all **grass centers** (back of north and south alcoves, 6" off fence, 32" apart).
7. Then mark all **front-line centers** (8" off front edge, 14" apart for coneflowers, 24" apart in the pear zone for geraniums).
8. Walk the bed. Sight down each row. Adjust flags before digging.

### Day 1 afternoon — Dig and plant (2–3 hrs)

9. Dig all grass holes first (16" × 14"). Pre-mix the backfill in a wheelbarrow: 50% native / 40% compost / 10% leaf mold or peat, plus one tablespoon of slow-release organic fertilizer per hole.
10. Set each grass at **½" above grade**, backfill, firm gently with hands, water in with 1–2 gallons immediately.
11. Dig front-line holes (10" × 10"). Same backfill mix. Coneflowers get a teaspoon of starter fertilizer; geraniums get none.
12. Plant coneflowers and geraniums at ½" above grade, backfill, firm, water in with ~1 gallon each.

### Day 2 morning — Mulch and deep-water (1.5 hrs)

13. Spread 3" of shredded hardwood mulch across the entire bed footprint. Pull mulch 2" away from the base of each plant.
14. Deep-water the entire bed with a soaker hose for 45–60 minutes, or by hand-watering ~50 gallons across the full run.
15. Position the climbing dome wherever works for the kids; the straight bed edge gives flexibility for placement.

## 7. Year-1 Watering Schedule

The original plan's "2–3× per week deeply for 4 weeks" is roughly half of what this soil profile actually needs. Sand's available water capacity is ~1/3 of clay; the schedule has to compensate.

### Week 1 (planting week)

- **Day of planting:** 1 gal per coneflower/geranium, 2 gal per grass, watered in at the time of planting. Then a general bed soak for 20–30 minutes with hose at low flow.
- **Days 2, 4, 6:** Individual plant watering only — 1 gal each flower, 2 gal each grass.
- Best times: early morning (6–9 AM) or evening (after 6 PM). Avoid midday.

### Weeks 2–4 (establishment)

- **3× per week**, deep at each plant. Same volumes as week 1.
- Skip a watering only if you got ≥½" of rain in the preceding 24 hours. Use a rain gauge in the yard, not a weather app — your microclimate may differ.

### Weeks 5–8

- **2× per week**, deep. Roots are starting to extend beyond the amended planting hole. Letting the surface dry slightly between waterings encourages roots to chase moisture downward into the sub-sand.

### Weeks 9–12

- **1× per week**, really deep. Each event should put down ~1" across the bed — roughly 60 gallons for the full run, or 45 min on a soaker hose.

### Weeks 13+ through first frost

- **As needed**, generally every 7–10 days during dry stretches. Roots are now down in the sand profile and can reach moisture you can't easily deliver from the surface.

### Special rule for the pear zone

**Add one extra watering per week throughout year 1.** Pear roots will pull moisture out of the amended geranium holes faster than open-bed roots pull from theirs. The geraniums need the boost while they establish.

### Year 2

- Spring through end of May: usually no watering (residual soil moisture from snowmelt + spring rains)
- June–August: 1× per week deep during dry stretches (no rain >½" in 7 days)
- September onward: usually no watering

### Year 3+

- Established plants on this site should need water only during prolonged dry spells (2+ weeks no rain in summer)
- Grasses become essentially drought-tolerant; may go semi-dormant in extreme drought, recover with fall rains
- Coneflowers may wilt midday in extreme heat and recover at night — that's normal, don't water for it
- Geraniums signal need by leaves going floppy — water when you see this

### Delivery: how to actually apply water

**Recommended: soaker hose.** One 50 ft soaker snaked along the bed about 6" inside the front edge (between the flower row and the mulch front), running 30–60 minutes per session at low pressure. Bury under the mulch (UV degrades soaker hoses). Cost: $25–40 plus a hose Y-splitter.

**Acceptable: hand watering with a wand or watering can.** Be honest about whether you'll do it 3× weekly for 8 weeks. Keep a 5-gal bucket near the bed to make it easier.

**Skip: overhead sprinklers / sprayers.** On sand, overhead watering wastes ~40% to evaporation and encourages shallow rooting and foliar disease.

### How to tell if you're watering enough

The "finger test" is unreliable on sand because the surface dries while moisture remains at depth. Use:

**Screwdriver test:** Push a long screwdriver into the bed near a plant. It should slide in with steady resistance for 6–8". If it stops shallow, soil is dry below — water more. If it slides like the soil is mush, you're overwatering.

**Dig test (once a week):** Scrape back the mulch in one spot and dig a 4–6" deep hole. The soil should feel cool and slightly damp, not wet, not dry.

**Underwatering signs (most common on sand):**
- Morning wilting (afternoon wilt is normal in heat)
- New growth small or yellowed
- Coneflower leaf edges curling
- Grass tips browning before mid-summer

**Overwatering signs (less common but possible year 1):**
- Black mushy spots at the base of stems
- Yellowing leaves dropping from the bottom
- Persistent flushes of mushrooms in the mulch
- Mosquito larvae in standing water

### Honest expectations for year 1

- **5–10% loss rate is normal.** Plant the buffer extras you bought; sub them in when something dies.
- The first hot dry July week is when losses happen if they're going to. Over-attend that week.
- Karl Foerster may look unimpressive year 1 — short or no plumes. Normal. Year 2 it does its thing.
- Spring-planted coneflowers will bloom year 1 but lightly. Year 2 they perform.
- Geraniums won't spread much year 1; they're building roots. Year 2 they start filling in.

## 8. Maintenance Calendar

### Annual rhythm

| Month | Task |
|---|---|
| **Late Feb / early March** | Cut Karl Foerster to 4–6" with hedge shears or hand pruners *before* new growth emerges. Cut coneflower stalks back to 6". Clean dead leaves from geraniums but leave the rosettes alone. |
| **Mid-March** | **Spring flooding observation pass.** After the first heavy spring rain or melt, walk the bed. Note any spot where water sits >24 hours. Mark with a flag for follow-up. |
| **Late March / early April** | Pull winter weeds. Refresh mulch to 3" everywhere it's thinned. Sandy beds eat mulch faster than clay beds — expect ~1" added annually. |
| **Early April** | Light handful of slow-release organic fertilizer per grass; half-handful per coneflower; **none for geraniums**. Scratch into mulch surface, water in. |
| **Mid-May** | Screwdriver test for soil moisture. If April–May was dry, start light watering. |
| **June** | Karl Foerster plumes emerge mid-month. Coneflowers budding late June. Geraniums bloom pink/magenta late May to mid-June. |
| **July–August** | Watering is the main job (Section 7). Optionally deadhead spent coneflowers for tidier look and a second flush, or leave seedheads for finches. |
| **Late August** | If a bad drought, geranium leaves may yellow. Cut entire plant back to 3" if it's bad — it re-leafs within 3 weeks once temps drop. |
| **September** | Coneflower seedheads developing. Grass plumes turning wheat-tan. Both look intentional. |
| **October** | Stop watering. Last drink around first hard frost. |
| **November–February** | Do nothing. Wheat plumes against snow are the design payoff. Resist cutback urge. |

### Division schedule

**Karl Foerster — every 4–5 years, early spring (late March)**
- Sign it's time: center of clump goes hollow or sparse; plumes get shorter
- Method: lift whole clump with a fork, split with a sharp spade or saw, replant divisions at the same depth
- First division: spring of year 4 at earliest
- Yields 3–4 new plants per division

**Echinacea — usually doesn't need division, can divide every 4–5 years**
- May self-seed lightly on your sandy site (straight species more than 'Magnus')
- Year 3+ may see volunteer seedlings; move or pull them
- Division method: lift in early spring, cut crown with sharp knife into 3–4 sections each with roots and a growth point

**Geranium macrorrhizum — divide as soon as plants touch (year 3-ish)**
- They'll form a continuous mat by year 3 — that's the goal, stop dividing then
- For propagation: slice off rhizome chunks with a spade, replant or share. Spring or fall both work.

### Fertilizer reality

Sand-soil beds get over-fertilized by anxious gardeners. Signs you're overdoing it:
- Floppy grasses needing staking (Karl Foerster should *never* need staking)
- Coneflowers with weak stems and oversized leaves
- Excessive volunteer coneflower seedlings
- Geraniums looking lush but not blooming

If you see those, skip a year of feed.

### Never till

Do not till or cultivate the bed soil after year 1. Tilling on sand brings deeper sand up, buries the surface organic layer where decomposers can't reach it, slices through crowns and rhizomes, and triggers weed seed germination. If you need to add compost in year 3+, top-dress ½" under the mulch refresh — earthworms and roots do the incorporation.

## 9. Gotchas — Sandy Ridge Edition

Updated from the original plan for this site's actual conditions.

1. **Underwatering year 1.** The single biggest risk. Sand drains in hours, not days. Follow the Section 7 schedule even when the surface looks wet.
2. **Planting at grade, not proud.** ½" above surrounding bed level is non-negotiable on this site. Crowns sitting in spring-melt puddles rot.
3. **Mulch volcano against the crown.** Causes rot. Always 2" of clearance.
4. **Cutting Karl Foerster in fall.** Wheat plumes against winter snow are the whole point. Cut in March, not November.
5. **One exception to the above:** if you get a heavy wet November snow that flattens the grass into a mat against the mulch, cut it then. Flattened grass holding moisture against the crown all winter risks crown rot at spring thaw — exactly the failure mode this site is most vulnerable to.
6. **Tilling.** Don't. See section 8.
7. **Hybrid coneflowers ('Cheyenne Spirit' et al.).** Pretty for one season, then dead. Stick with straight species or 'Magnus'.
8. **Buying *Rudbeckia hirta*.** Biennial; will not be a permanent border plant. The original plan said this and it's still true.
9. **Crowding the pear.** Trying to plant up to the trunk produces sad plants and angry tree roots. The geranium zone honors the canopy edge.
10. **Plastic landscape edging (rolled black stuff).** Heaves out of sandy soil in 1–2 winters. Use steel, composite, or no edging.
11. **Skipping mulch year 1 to save money.** Non-negotiable on sand. Mulch is half the water-retention strategy.
12. **Cheap big-box "compost".** Mostly bark fines; gives you no CEC. Buy real mushroom compost or composted manure.
13. **Over-fertilizing.** Sand-soil gardeners' instinct is "more nutrients needed." Actually less, because over-feeding causes worse problems than under-feeding here.
14. **Ignoring spring flooding observations.** If one spot ponds >24 hours repeatedly, that's the bed telling you something. Either lift that section's grade with extra compost+sand, or swap those plants for a sedge (*Carex pensylvanica* is the right native for transient wet on this soil/zone).
15. **Treating the pear canopy as a problem to fight.** The geranium zone makes it an asset. Don't backslide into trying to plant coneflowers there because they're cheaper.

## 10. Year 2–3 Look

**End of year 1:**
- Karl Foerster: short plumes or none. Looks like the bed is missing its top half. Normal.
- Coneflowers: blooming but sparse, ~2 ft tall.
- Geraniums: small green mounds, slow spread.
- Bed reads as "newly planted" — visible mulch dominates.

**End of year 2:**
- Karl Foerster: 4 ft plumes, holding all winter.
- Coneflowers: dense 2.5–3 ft mass of bloom from late June through September.
- Geraniums: starting to spread, ~⅔ coverage of the pear zone.
- Bed reads as established but not yet mature.

**End of year 3:**
- Karl Foerster: full 4–5 ft plumes.
- Coneflowers: textbook prairie border, dense bloom, attracting goldfinches.
- Geraniums: continuous mat under pear, semi-evergreen.
- Bed reads as a **continuous green-and-purple ribbon** along the entire fence line, with the pear tree as the anchor at the north end.

**Year 4 onward:**
- First Karl Foerster division (if needed) in spring.
- Bed essentially self-maintaining: spring cleanup, mulch refresh, occasional weeding. The watering load drops to "drought-only."

## 11. Quick Reference Summary

- **Plants:** 12 Karl Foerster grass (buy 14) + 24 Echinacea purpurea (buy 27) + 5 Geranium macrorrhizum (buy 6). **Total 41 plants, buy 47.**
- **Layout:** Straight front edge running 549" along property line. Grasses against fence in north (5) and south (7) alcoves only. Front line: coneflowers in sun, geraniums under pear canopy at north jut corner.
- **Spacing:** Grasses 32" apart, 6" off fence. Coneflowers 14" apart, 8" off front edge. Geraniums 24" apart on same front line.
- **Soil:** Excessively drained sand below 10" loam cap. Amend each hole 50/40/10 (native/compost/leaf mold). Broadfork the pear zone bed-wide.
- **Crown placement:** ½" above grade — spring flooding adjustment.
- **Mulch:** 3" shredded hardwood, 2" clearance around crowns.
- **Year-1 watering:** 3× weekly weeks 2–4, taper to 1× weekly by week 12. Pear zone gets one extra weekly watering all year 1. Soaker hose recommended.
- **Don't:** Till, fall-cut grasses, plant deep, use cheap compost, over-fertilize.
- **Do:** Top-dress with compost in year 3+, refresh mulch annually, divide grasses every 4–5 years.
